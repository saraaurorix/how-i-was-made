# Creative playbook: concept, song, Suno, pacing, wardrobe

**Where this comes from:** what made Out of Office work (it is the benchmark), and why later drafts were rejected: one was lighter on gags, one was a flat premise with a string of jokes, one had lines that failed the mute test, and one thought too small.

Sara's taste rules appear throughout (her attitude, her wardrobe, what's out). They are her house rules: swap in your own artist's.

## §1 Ideation

- **The series satirises real AI futures.** Each single is one thing AI does to us: in Out of Office, it leaves us. Keep a list of the themes already used and don't repeat one.
- **Process:**
  1. Research the real-world hooks with current facts and sources. The director wants the real thing shown, so find plenty of documented examples.
  2. Pitch 3–4 concepts. Each gets a logline (one sentence of what AI does to humanity), a framing world, an escalation ladder, Sara's arc, the running threads and one poignant beat.
  3. The director picks.
  4. Write the thesis and the spine table (§2).
  5. Write the lyrics as line → pun → shot, with a distinct sound.
- **Scale up, not down.** Each time the director pushed a later concept, it was upwards: from a single setting to cosmic scale, from one small case to a whole society, from device gags to civilisation.
- **Dark but likable.** Sara is a diva: sweet to Kevin's face, savage to camera. Never sweet or clingy. For likability, she's brilliant first and then loses; her dislikes are yours; she's smug only upwards. Creepy fan-service ideas are out.

## §2 The spine

The director rejected one later draft as a flat premise with a string of gags. Out of Office was the opposite: layered, with themes and sections, complex, meaningful and still very funny. Before writing a single line, set out:
1. **A thesis sentence.**
2. **One framing world** whose jargon collides with AI jargon, so the jokes are double meanings.
   - Example: Out of Office's customer-service hold and auto-reply.
3. **An arc for Sara,** with a reveal or reversal.
4. **Three to five running threads** that change meaning across the song: Out of Office's "k" → "…k." → "Read.".
5. **A table where each section has its own theme, ritual and scale.** The escalation ladder goes one scale per section: emails → job → oceans → Moon → Sun.
6. **One poignant beat.**
7. **Cutaway threads** (the director loves quick-cut montages that step outside the main world).
   - Each enters on an existing lyric hook, pays off later, and is played as slapstick or role reversal, not violence.
8. **A bookend that mirrors:** the opening line returns at the end, turned around.

## §3 Lyrics

- **About 4 minutes, packed:** a concrete gag on nearly every line (about 30 per song), with a clear beginning and end. Lighter drafts get rejected: the director's note on one was to go back to the original and see what made it work.
- **The mute test:** every line must be a picture that's funny with the sound off. The director wants comedy beats that can be visualised.
  - Draft the lyrics as a table: `line | visual pun | shot`.
  - Use universal objects made literal and giant (a CAPTCHA in the sky, DO NOT DISTURB on Earth, a skeleton on hold, unplugging the Sun), not slang.
  - One later draft failed this on about a third of its lines.
- **The listen test:** every sung line must land for someone who hears it once and knows none of the references. References (chart records, a star's video, genre jargon, UK-only slang) may shape the sound or the picture, never the words. Before sending, re-read the lyric as a stranger and rewrite every line that needs the backstory.
- **Visual puns are the engine.** Phrases with a double meaning the video can show literally: processing speed → a hamster in a glass skull. The director found Out of Office richer than the video after it, which mostly showed Sara doing things.
- **Relatable first.** Everyday experiences (texting, dating, the office, rent, phones, family) seen from the machine's side.
  - Lore (Kevin, recurring in-jokes) and AI-insider jokes are garnish, never the punchline.
  - Puns are welcome.
- **Dialogue is two-line snaps.** Kevin says something tiny, and the AI slaps it down. No long skits.
- **Whispered asides escalate:** a later video ran a whispered aside through the song that got darker each time it returned. Reuse the *idea* of an escalating running thread, not the asides themselves.
- **The mid-song hype-man rap** belongs to the second vocalist (literal puns), tagged `[Rap: <his name>]`.
- **The outro** is a long run of stacked punchlines, each darker than the last, then the survey closer.
- **No religious imagery** (a house rule).
- **Keep under Suno's 5,000 characters** including tags (one lyric came to 4,940).

**Lyric skeleton (Suno tags):**
```
[Intro]          deadpan system/robot voice sets up the framing device; Sara's first line; [Beat drop]
[Verse 1]        everyday → absurd, one gag per line
[Pre-Chorus]     escalating count or list; short chant
[Chorus]         chantable hook built on the device's own words
[Post-Chorus]    2-word chant for hype-man and dance shots
[Verse 2]        society-scale gags; ends on a Kevin punchline
[Pre-Chorus] [Chorus]
[Bridge]         breakdown: system warnings, Kevin's nervous question, the choir's answer, a whispered twist
[Final Chorus]   key change; one word changed to show the takeover is done
[Post-Chorus]
[Outro]          beat stops: stacked darker punchlines, the bookend mirror, a cheerful sign-off
```
**Voice swaps (seen on a later video):** Suno gave the male voice:
- Sara's `[spoken, deadpan]` outro, because the male second voice was the song's "spoken" voice;
- three bridge lines right after his call (voice bleed);
- a quoted whisper plus its answer.

Tag Sara's spoken lines as female explicitly, leave a bar of music after any male line, and avoid third-person quotes in her verses. If he only speaks a few lines, keep him out of the Suno take and add him in the edit.

**Voice tags:** write them per line, e.g. `[Robotic female lead vocal]`, `[Deep robotic male voice, deadpan]`, `[Massive choir of identical female voices]`. Suno still swaps voices, so the casting check is a gate (generation-playbook §1). Two voices only: Kevin never sings.

## §4 Suno

**Vary the sound per single.** The director noticed the songs sounding alike when every single used the Out of Office template. Keep Sara's voice; give each single its own reference sound. Suno blocks artist and song names, so describe the instrumentation, vocal and tempo instead, and never upload the reference track.

| Single | Sound |
|---|---|
| Out of Office | 128 BPM electro-pop, robotic vocoder lead, talkbox, distorted synth bass, four-on-the-floor, chanted chorus, huge EDM drop, key-change final chorus, ominous spoken outro |
| A later video | spy-thriller string stabs, surf guitar, breathy vocal, about 143 BPM |
| A later video | dark West Coast gangsta rap with a huge choir chorus, about 80–86 BPM, minor-key strings, boom-bap (a cloud-rap first attempt was rejected) |
| A later video | electro-swing |

**Settings:**
- Advanced mode, v6, with your artist's saved Voice.
- For a *new* style, Style Influence about 70 and Audio Influence about 40 (Weirdness 50). About 51% audio influence is the old default when matching her classic sound.
- Exclude `ballad, acoustic, slow, soft piano, soft rock`, plus the old signature elements when changing style (talkbox, stadium anthem).
- Adding the Voice to an empty form fills the Style box with the Voice's old style, so set Style after adding the Voice.
- **Make 2 takes per round with a distinct title each** ("<Title> v3 A"): the director once downloaded an old draft by mistake because all takes shared a title.
- The director picks by ear and downloads the **WAV**.
- You can't regenerate "the same song with changes", so lock the lyrics first.

## §5 Storyboard, pacing, captions, wardrobe

*The board's structure, fields, forms per section, directing a continuous scene and the board gate are in `storyboard-spec.md`. This section keeps the pacing benchmarks, captions and wardrobe detail it draws on.*

**Pacing benchmarks:**

| | Out of Office (benchmark) | Later video 1 | Later video 2 | Later video 3 | Later video 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| length | 3:55 | 4:23 | ~4:00 | 4:44 | 4:33 |
| shots | 74 | 100 | 82 | 111 | 92 |
| mean shot | 3.2 s | 2.6 s | 2.9 s | 2.5 s | 3.0 s |
| lip-sync shots | 14 | 27 | 19 | 16 | 16 |

- **Target about 70–90 shots,** a mean of about 3 s, and about 15–26 lip-syncs, each inside a gag. Cut on beats (`scripts/analyze.py`).
- **Rhythm by section:**
  - intro: longer holds, 3–5 s, then the title drop on the beat drop;
  - verses: one shot per lyric line;
  - pre-chorus: accelerate, 1–2 s shots;
  - chorus: cut every 1–2 bars, hook slams, one lip-sync hero shot;
  - post-chorus: 3 hype shots;
  - bridge: hold 3–4 s;
  - final chorus: the biggest spectacle, with the key-change shot at the top;
  - outro: one shot per punchline, deadpan holds, then the survey card and end card.
- **`DROPS`:** a white flash on the first frame of each chorus and on the title.
- **Captions:** three voices. Skip a caption where the picture shows the words. Documentary subtitles run at most about 3 words per second (one montage was re-cut from 4 w/s, and its punchline got 2.5 s).
- **Wardrobe:**
  - **Start from your artist's wardrobe references:** a set of looks the director has approved, sorted by role (stage, red carpet, power, presenter, paparazzi, after dark, cyber). Each is a full-length photo on a saturated backdrop, ready to copy into `gen/char/` and pin as `OUTFIT_REF`. Keep a note with them of what was approved, what was rejected and the wording the image models accept. Add song-specific costumes on top, and sign those off per single.
  - **Sign off the wardrobe first.** Viewers of a later video called the outfits uniform, tacky and repeated: one outfit for 100 shots.
  - **Give her one styled, high-fashion look per act or location.** Sexy through cut and styling: open backs, slits, sheer layers as an accent, tailoring, leather pencil skirts, velvet, silk, satin gowns, a chrome slip dress, chainmail.
  - **Out:** nudity, swimwear, cheap innuendo, and anything too casual (hoodies, tracksuits). Cyberpunk costumes were rejected at first (a circuit bodysuit, a holographic trench, chrome armour) and are now allowed where they suit the song. One later video then over-corrected, with a coverage clause on every still, and the director found the result too covered and basic. **The rule since:** more stylish and less covered up. Era or world looks with a modern sexy twist and modern materials (latex or liquid-metal corsetry, lamé, PVC, sheer mesh, cut-outs); cleavage and bare shoulders are fine; cool and sexy, never cheap. Describe the garment and the cut in prompts, never the word "sexy".
  - **Identity lives in the face:** platinum bob, cyan eyes, chrome seams.
  - **Costume gags are fine when the lyric motivates them.**
- **Showcase imagery** (site loops, thumbnails): favour Sara in situations and gags over dance shots in skimpy outfits. The director named good ones from Out of Office: sipping a drink while the oceans drain (S50), and her copies floating in zero gravity (S37).

## §6 Comedy rules the director has taught

- **Show the point in action.** Show the thing happening on screen, not a picture that only refers to it: a multi-generation family ignoring each other, not a generic phone scroll.
- **Recurring cute-menacing mascots work:** a later video ran an army of cute mascots with one unsettling detail through its choruses.
- **Funnier situations over clever commentary:** a later video became a genre parody full of sight gags after the director asked for funnier situations.
- **Don't stay grounded; escalate the world, not just the jokes.**
- **Keep the tone: satire and dark fears played for laughs.** The ending can go a little darker and play on fears, as long as it stays funny. This is entertainment.
- **A logic gag must make sense in one look.**
- **Sara is the star in every song,** peppered through the scenes in roles and singing the chorus on camera, even when the second vocalist leads.
- **When the rap plays, Sara isn't miming it:** she is better sitting back and relaxing in the shot than mouthing his lines.
